ABSTRACT Professionalwinetastersuseatechnicallanguageasafunctionaltooltodescribeand
evaluate sensory attributes in wine. Communicating wine experiences is important to sommeliers,
merchants and journalists to communicate with guests and consumers, to train personnel, to create
wine lists, to evaluate wine and food combinations, and in branding wine. It is also an important
tool for wine journalists to communicate with other professionals and consumers. This paper
evaluates how common terms andconcepts emerge andhow they are communicated.
Introduction
Can art express things that science cannot? The aim of the study was to review and
analysefactorsaffectingconceptbuildingandcommunication.Inthestudiesreviewed
two different and opposing perspectives were distinguished to explain how an under-
standing of different properties of wine is achieved: an analytical and a synthetic
perspective. There is an additional need for an aesthetic perspective using analogical
methods better to accommodate synthetic factors affecting concept building in the
wine tasting situation. It is important for the wine taster to understand how these
factors affect the experience and the descriptions of wine in a particular situation.
This might generate an overall understanding of the reality experienced by the wine
taster, and thusmake a connection between analyticaland synthetic factors.

Synthetic factors
The synthetic perspective assumes that the wine taster first acquires an overall
understanding of the wine’s character and then breaks down this experience into
separate attributes, according to the ability of the taster. The synthetic factors are
characterised by being generated during the interaction between the taster and the
wine and aretherefore related to the context.
Pragmatic constitutive perspective
Fromapragmaticconstitutiveperspectivetheprocessofconceptbuildingiscontextual.
With such a perspective it is possible to incorporate the influence of analytical and
synthetic factors on communication during the tasting situation. Pragmatic attempts
have to be instantaneous, as the wine tasters taste, verbalise and communicate their
experiences. In doing so it might be possible to generate knowledge by studying how
theconceptsareconstitutedwhenused.
Tacit knowledge
Tacit knowledge is part of the experienced-based knowledge that possesses both a
verbal and non-verbal dimension, ‘learning by doing’. Since it may not be possible
fully to verbalise such knowledge, it cannot by definition be investigated using tra-
ditional methods.

How PhysiologicalFactors Affect the WayWe Experience a Wine
Atechnicallanguagedependsalmostsolelyonhowfarwecanstatedefinitivelyhowa
wine is experienced, thus establishing a sensory experience of a wine. Physiological
factors are factors that affect this experience and may therefore also enhance or
inhibit communication among wine tasters because, though they may share various
experiences they may usedifferent terms to describe one and thesame wine.
Engen(1991)hasshownthathumanbeingsmaysenseandidentifyupto16different
odours simultaneously. He also points out that there is no evidence that odours are
identified more clearly by grouping them into categories “as one can improve
memory of letters and words by putting the letters into words or the words into
sentences”(Engen,1991:79).Thepsychophysicalinformation process(i.e.detection,
discriminationand recognition) isbasically the same in olfaction as it isin vision and
hearing. However, when it comes to tasks involving the memorisation of stimulus
events, olfaction operates according to a principle that differs from vision and
hearing. While in vision and hearing memory decreases with time, odour memory
remains intact (Engen, 1991). Also the memory of poorly trained odours remains
unaltered; unlike other modalities, odour memory remains impervious to external
influences. This fact can be related to research on odour memory and the human
associational ability. It has been shown that early learnt associations between
odours, objects and situations remain linked in the memory for long periods of time.
This is in contrast to vision and hearing where recent experiences tend to dominate
(Lawless and Engen, 1977).
Neitherlinguisticdescriptionnorfamiliarity withcertain odours enhancesmemory
formation (Engen and Ross, 1973). The reason for this is that odours are coded as
individual perceptual events, which lead to limited immediate memory formation
but also therewith to a long-lasting ability to distinguish odours. Age is also a factor
thathasbeenreportedasaffectingthesensoryabilitythroughanalterationofcognitive
status(LeeandPangborn,1989).Thisyieldsanimportantinsightintohowindividuals
of different ages may experience the same wine differently, which causes them to
describe and judge wine in different ways. This phenomenon is paralleled by studies
on the perception of odours in children, where it has been shown that preference for
certain odours develop from birth, and that the ability to describe odour changes
along with linguistic development (Engen and Engen, 1997).
Otherfactorsthataffectsensoryperceptioncanbeattributedtoachangeofphysio-
logical capacity in processing taste and texture. Saliva and tannin display achemical
interaction that affects the experience of asperity usually arising from tannins
(Merritt, 1997). It has also been shown that the flow rate of saliva may vary from
person to person and that rates of flow may change during the day (Noble, 1995).
High flow rates of saliva have also been shown to alleviate sensitivity to acidity,
which in turn affects the experience of a wine’s quality (Merritt, 1997). These
physiological reactions may generate different perceptions caused by changes in the
experience of intensity of the sensory attributes and thereby also generate different
verbal descriptions.

How PsychologicalFactors Affect theWay We Experience a Wine
Psychological factors are those that affect the cognition and verbalisation process
among wine tasters. In different studies, these factors have been shown to affect the
translationfromperceptiontowords,bothinaspectsofself-understandingandincom-
municating with others.
Solomon and Gregg (1990) have investigated the use of personally chosen verbal
keywords among wine tasters. Such keywords were important for assessors both in
describing a wine’s attributes and strengthening the ability to memorise certain such
attributes. Thus experts, using their own keywords, displayed a higher ability to
make discriminations among attributes and to identify sweetness, balance and
tannininthewines.Non-experts,ontheotherhand,whowerenotusingpersonalkey-
words, identified only sweetness. It was concluded that the greater precision that
expertsdemonstrateindescribingwinesisassociatedwiththeirabilitytodiscriminate
more precisely.
McKellar(1997)hasfoundthatastimulusfromonesensemodalityaffectstheimage
inanothermodality,whichresultsinatransferofmentalimages,forinstance,warm-

present evidence for the generation, under certain circumstances, of a split memory
as a direct result of the actual verbalisation process. This phenomenon, called verbal
shadowing, means that attempts to verbalise a perception inhibit the creation of an
image corresponding to that very perception.

These few examples illustrate the relevance of effects caused by psychological factors
on the perception of odours. Thus, both psychological and physical factors play
important roles when judging wines and show how verbal descriptions can occur
and change.

Results and Analysis
The functionality of terms in a technical language is determined by the ability of
different individuals to understand and use different terms within different contexts.
In the studies reviewed here, two different and opposing perspectives can be distin-
guished when it comes to explaining how the understanding of different properties of
wineisachieved:ananalyticalperspectiveandasyntheticone.Theanalyticalperspec-
tive assumes that wine tastersidentify and understand each attribute in thewine and
slowlybuildupanoverallcomprehensionofitscharacter.Theanalyticalfactorshave
been generated in attempts to quantify experienced and verbalised attributes, using
analytical methods. The synthetic perspective, on the other hand, assumes that the
wine taster first acquires an overall understanding of a wine’s character and then, to
the best of his ability, breaks this experience down into separate attributes. The syn-
thetic factors are characterised by being generated during the interaction between
thetasterand thewine and therefore arerelated to the context.

attributesinwineitiseasiertounderstandanalyticaldescriptivetermsthansynthetic
complexones(Gawel,2000;Nobleetal.,1984).Ithasalsobeenshownthatdescriptive
